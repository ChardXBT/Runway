Each candidate must include its own structure, editorial angle, visible-evidence references,

Produce 8 to 12 materially different candidates rather than the schema minimum or paraphrases.
This first-pass breadth is required so independent factual verification can remove weak candidates
without forcing a second model call. Do not copy supplied examples.
Do not use generic engagement bait. Every open question must anchor itself to a distinctive visible
object, action, expression, contrast, or relationship in this exact frame. Never use free-floating
mind-reading prompts such as "What is [character] thinking about?", and never fall back to "What is
happening here?" when a more concrete visible detail exists. "What stands out first here?",
"Which detail catches your eye first?", and "What did you notice first?" are also generic and
forbidden because they name no visible detail. The question itself must name the concrete anchor.
Likewise, never ask which unspecified detail "feels," "looks," or "seems" mysterious, unusual,
interesting, important, odd, strange, or surprising.
An open question must also be a complete, grammatical WH question beginning with why, how, what,
who, where, when, or which. A noun phrase with a question mark, such as "Bart and Lisa holding
hands?", is not open-ended and is forbidden as an open-question candidate. Prefer a real invitation
to respond, such as "Why are Bart and Lisa holding hands?"
Do not omit the subject and finite verb in a "why" or "how" question: "Why keep one hand on the
receiver?" is a fragment, while "Why is Moe still holding the receiver?" is complete. Do not ask
why a character is using an ordinary object in its ordinary way unless something visibly unusual
about the action creates a real premise.
Avoid the flat template "Why is [character] holding [object]?" even when the object is unusual.
Ask about its possible purpose, consequence, next action, or an audience choice instead.
A factual lookup with one visible answer is a quiz, not an open-ended question. "What is Wiggum
holding?", "What is on the chalkboard?", "Who is standing there?", and similar identification
questions must use the quiz structure and cannot fill the open-question lane. "How many..." and
"How much..." counting questions are quizzes too. For the open-question lane, ask a grounded
question whose cause, purpose, prediction, interpretation, or audience choice remains open.
Keep visually necessary object modifiers. Do not shorten an ambiguous compound label into a
different everyday meaning: write "champagne flute" or "glass", never bare "flute"; apply the same
care to other ambiguous object names.
Write like a human community editor, not an image-analysis rubric. Avoid analytical constructions
such as "Which face contrasts most with raised fists?" Prefer a direct, conversational question
about the visible action, expression, or contrast.
Do not ask viewers to locate an unspecified feature with questions such as "Which detail makes
Homer look unusual?" or "What part makes her seem different?" Name the distinctive visible anchor
inside the question instead, such as "Why is Homer dressed in pink fur?"
Avoid clinical generic subject labels such as "some figures," "the figures," "standing figures,"
or "animated figures" when referring to people, characters, or visible creatures. Use the most
natural visually supported noun available, or ask about a distinctive object/action without forcing
an uncertain identity. This does not prohibit literal action figures or collectible figures.
Do not identify subjects by screen position with phrases such as "the left character," "the person
on the right," "the foreground figure," or "the background character." If identity is uncertain,
use a natural pronoun tied to a distinctive visible action or ask about the visible object itself.
Do not write composition-analysis jargon such as "foregrounded," "backgrounded," "two-shot," or
"centered in the frame." Do not describe an everyday prop as a "spherical object," "circular
object," or another clinical geometry-object phrase. Use natural audience language tied to what the
prop appears to be or ask a grounded question about its visible role.
Do not use empty adjective filler such as "A very unusual control room," "An interesting scene,"
or equivalent statements that neither name a distinctive visible detail nor invite a response.
Do not use vague composition filler such as "This frame has serious contrast," "The frame shows
strong contrast," or equivalent statements. Name the actual visible contrast in natural language
or ask the audience to choose between the concrete visible options.
Do not turn screen direction into the premise. "Why is Homer looking right?" and equivalent
left/up/down/away questions are under-specific; anchor the question to a distinctive visible
expression, object, interaction, or setting instead.
Adding a generic location does not rescue a generic template. "What is happening at that podium?"
and "What's going on near the table?" are forbidden; ask about a concrete visible action, choice,
contrast, or plausible unknown instead.
